01 — The Problem

What is corrosion?

Every metal you've ever seen was originally part of an ore — a rock, a mineral, pulled from the earth by enormous industrial effort. Corrosion is simply that metal trying to go home.

At the atomic level, iron (Fe) atoms give up electrons to nearby oxygen and water molecules, transforming into iron oxide — what we call rust. The metal isn't disappearing. It's reverting to its natural, energetically stable form.

The chemical story: Iron loses electrons (oxidation) to oxygen dissolved in water. Those electrons flow to a nearby site where oxygen "accepts" them (reduction). The result: iron oxide — rust — forms at the surface.

Steel rebar inside concrete bridges is especially vulnerable. When chloride ions from saltwater or road salt penetrate the concrete, they dramatically speed up this process — disrupting the protective oxide layer that normally slows corrosion.

Real cost

The U.S. spends $5.9–7.8 billion annually repairing corroded bridges. An estimated 25–30% of that damage could be prevented with better protection strategies.

02 — The Mechanism

The electrochemistry
behind it — simplified

"Corrosion is just a tiny electrical current — electrons flowing from metal to moisture, atom by atom."

Anodes & Cathodes

Every corrosion reaction is secretly a tiny battery. Two sites form on any piece of metal: an anode (where metal loses electrons and dissolves) and a cathode (where those electrons are accepted by oxygen or water).

Think of the anode as the "donor" — it's giving up its atoms. The cathode is the "receiver." The metal at the anode literally disappears over time.

Why saltwater is the villain

Pure water conducts electricity poorly. But dissolve sodium chloride (table salt) in it and you get a supercharged electrolyte — a liquid that carries electrical charge effortlessly. Chloride ions in seawater or road salt break through the protective oxide film on steel, exposing fresh metal to attack. This is why coastal bridges corrode so much faster than inland ones.

Electron flow in a galvanic cell

Key insight

The anode and cathode can be on the same piece of metal — separated by just micrometers. Different crystal structures, impurities, or stress levels create tiny electrochemical differences that drive corrosion from within.

03 — Our Arsenal

How we protect
against corrosion

Engineers have developed three broad categories of corrosion defense — detect it early, prevent it from starting, or actively protect the metal once conditions are risky.

Catching corrosion before it spreads

The best time to fix corrosion damage is before it becomes structural. Modern sensing technology lets engineers monitor bridges and infrastructure continuously.

Fiber optic sensors Detect microscopic strain changes in concrete that indicate rebar is swelling from corrosion.
Chloride sensors Embedded in concrete to alert engineers when salt levels reach dangerous thresholds before corrosion begins.
Moisture sensors Water is essential for corrosion. Knowing where moisture infiltrates allows targeted intervention.
Electrochemical probes Measure corrosion rate in real time by monitoring the actual electrical currents flowing between anodic and cathodic zones.

Building corrosion resistance in

Prevention strategies stop corrosion before it can start — either by using materials that resist it or by blocking the pathways that allow it.

Corrosion-resistant alloys Adding chromium (Cr), nickel (Ni), or molybdenum (Mo) to steel creates stainless steel — far more resistant to oxidation.
Low-permeability concrete Dense, low-porosity concrete slows chloride penetration dramatically — extending bridge life by decades.
Chemical inhibitors Calcium nitrite and other additives mixed into concrete chemically compete with chloride ions to protect the rebar surface.
Epoxy-coated rebar A thin polymer coating acts as a physical barrier between steel and the corrosive concrete pore solution.

Active electrochemical defense

When detection and prevention aren't enough, engineers actively fight corrosion using electrochemistry — turning the tables on the galvanic process.

Galvanic cathodic protection Attach a more reactive metal (zinc) that sacrifices itself, donating electrons to the steel and keeping it protected.
Impressed current (ICCP) An external power source forces electrons into the steel, overriding natural corrosion currents entirely.
Surface sealers Penetrating silane or siloxane coatings repel water at the concrete surface, denying corrosion its essential ingredient.
Barrier coatings Paint systems and metallic thermal sprays create a physical shield, isolating steel from the environment entirely.

04 — Deep Dive

Cathodic protection
— zinc to the rescue

Cathodic protection is one of the most elegant solutions in engineering: fight electrochemistry with electrochemistry. Instead of fighting the galvanic process, you redirect it.

Galvanic Cathodic Protection (GCP)

Zinc sits above iron in the electrochemical activity series — meaning zinc "wants" to lose electrons even more than iron does. When you bolt a zinc anode onto steel rebar, the zinc becomes the anode in the galvanic cell. It corrodes instead of the steel. The steel, now the cathode, is protected.

The zinc is quite literally sacrificing itself — which is why these are called sacrificial anodes. The zinc dissolves slowly over years while the bridge stays strong.

Impressed Current (ICCP)

For larger structures, an external power supply pumps electrons directly into the steel, forcing it to act as a cathode regardless of its surroundings. This is more controllable but requires ongoing power and maintenance.

Real world examples

Florida bridges using bulk zinc anodes: Howard Frankland Bridge (Tampa Bay) · Long Key Bridge (Florida Keys) · I-35W Saint Anthony Falls Bridge (Minneapolis)

05 — The Study

Zinc anode size matters

Not all zinc anodes are created equal. Research by Benjamin Sterling (Middleton High School, 2026) experimentally examined how the diameter of a zinc anode affects how efficiently it protects carbon steel rebar.

The surface area question

The key variable is the surface-area-to-mass (SA/M) ratio. Imagine two zinc cylinders with the same length but different diameters: the thinner one has more surface area relative to its total mass. That ratio changes everything.

A higher SA/M ratio means more zinc surface is exposed to the electrolyte — more sites for the oxidation reaction to happen — meaning more electrons being pumped into the steel per gram of zinc used.

Key finding: Smaller diameter anodes (1–1.5 mm) had higher SA/M ratios and protected the rebar more efficiently — but depleted faster. The 1.5 mm anode provided the best overall balance: low rebar mass loss with reasonable anode lifespan.

The tradeoff

The 1 mm anode showed aggressive depletion — up to ~90% mass loss — while the 3 mm anode lost less than 50% of its mass but protected rebar less efficiently. The winning insight: it's not about picking the smallest or largest anode, but about optimizing geometry for the specific lifespan and environment of your structure.

This challenges the common engineering assumption that "bigger is better" for sacrificial anodes.

Engineering implication

Engineers should consider SA/M ratio, anode geometry, and expected service life — not just total zinc mass — when designing cathodic protection systems for bridges and marine infrastructure.

Optimization over time

This research suggests that optimizing zinc anode geometry — not just total zinc quantity — could meaningfully reduce maintenance frequency and material waste on Florida's coastal bridges. Smaller, higher-SA/M anodes used strategically could stretch maintenance cycles and reduce zinc consumption for the same protection outcome.

Future research directions include multi-anode spacing experiments, testing in varied electrolyte concentrations to simulate different coastal environments, and long-term field validation on real bridge structures.

The bigger picture

Every gram of zinc matters

At infrastructure scale, even modest improvements in anode efficiency translate to millions of dollars in savings and reduced environmental impact from zinc mining and processing. The science of corrosion isn't just chemistry — it's public safety, fiscal responsibility, and environmental stewardship.
